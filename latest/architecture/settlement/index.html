    Settlement

    What ack, retry, retry_after and drop become on SQS: each outcome is one call against the queue, and a retry waits in the queue itself.

    retry_after(d) counts the delay in whole seconds, rounded up, so the message never comes back before d. A delay that reaches past the 12 hours from the receive is shortened to end there, and the crate logs a warning that names the queue. A delivery dropped without a settlement makes no call: its extension stops, and the message returns when its current timeout lapses.

    SQS has no verb that rejects a message without deleting it, so drop deletes. A queue whose registration declares a redrive policy is the exception. There, a delivery received maxReceiveCount times is returned instead of deleted, because the next receive is what moves it to the dead-letter queue, and a delete would lose it.

    The delivery's redelivery_count is the queue's ApproximateReceiveCount, and the first delivery counts as one. It counts every receive, a lapsed timeout included, not only the retries the service asked for.

    Retries​

    A retry never leaves the queue. retry_after(d) makes the message invisible for d, and it comes back as the same message, with the same id and its receive count one higher. Nothing is republished, so the runtime's republish fallback never runs here, and .out_retry(..) does not compile on an SqsQueue. SQS refuses a visibility that ends more than 12 hours after the receive.

    max_attempts(n) and dead_letter(q) together are the queue's redrive policy: maxReceiveCount is n and the dead-letter queue is q. The subscription writes the policy onto the queue when it opens, and SQS counts the receives and moves a spent message itself. One half without the other is SqsError::IncompleteRedrive at startup.

    The queue holds a waiting message, so a crash of the service loses no retry: the message comes back when its visibility ends, to whichever consumer polls next.
